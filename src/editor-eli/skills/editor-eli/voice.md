### 5. The scope law (draft)

Giving AI a large project risks it failing since that becomes a lot of context for it to manage. The more data you give it, the more likely a hallucination will accrue.

Focusing the AI to build a simple part that can be tested for assurance of success seems like a great way to “fire and forget”. Fire and forget means we take the time to build the module we need, and test it, so that we can trust it to work well in the future when it gets plugged into other parts of the system.

I think a module at its lowest level is a function. A function has input and output. By changing functions together, we can create pretty sophisticated software.

The next layer up is a group of related functions. This is what I would typically consider a module. A module has public and internal functions. When a module is being created, we need to know which functions will be external so we can have specific tests for those external functions of the module.

### 6. Formal — Smith, the hand, and a thumb-war

While Smith is the father of economics, not all great economists would accept his ideas. Smith believed that without government meddling, economic actors make society much better through bartering (Buchholz 29). This is the basic tenet of classical economics, and other great economists like Ricardo and Marshall share similar ideologies. Marshall, however, believed that natural monopolies should be regulated and incentivized by the government (Buchholz 134). However, Keynes, Marx, and Schumpeter had different views on the effect that the “invisible hand” has on society. In fact, we will see how these economists saw Smith’s free-market “invisible hand” act more like an “invisible whacking stick” to society if unchecked.

Specifically, the business cycle’s ups and downs shows the “invisible hand” can get out of hand if nothing is done at all. The Great Depression is the most notorious example. Keynes believed that in such cases, it was actually the government's duty to declare a thumb-war with the “invisible hand” and provide economic incentives to get the macro economy back on track (Buchholz 170).

### 8. Formal — Marshall and a box of Chex

Marshall believed the value of a consumable was determined by the cost of labor and the utility the consumable gave to the buyer (Buchholz 130). Rational people, to Marshall, adjusted their preferences to whatever brought them the most value (Buchholz 131). If actors are able to adjust their preferences at any given moment, then demand for a product or service can change since actors decide whether or not it fits their fancy. If consumers decide that the box of Chex cereal is no longer worth $3.50, then, ceteris paribus, the seller will have to lower the price of Chex until the consumers decide the cereal is worth the price. An analogy such as this agrees with Smith’s “invisible hand” (Buchholz 30).

Marshall's economic model of the rational and marginal consumer explains many situations in micro economics. It is also the foundation of elasticity which quantifies how consumers change their behavior as they reevaluate the expected costs and utility of a given product or service. However, this model of the marginal consumers seems to expect economic actors to act quickly. In actuality, people and firms are often bound by contracts or other factors that limit the degree at which actors can change their consumption preferences.

### 9. Formal — Keynes and the ox

The only entity that would not be shattered by a deep recession is the government, so Keynes declared that it was up to the government to save the economy (Buchholz 160). This is almost the exact opposite of what Smith believed.

I have no doubt that in the short run, Keynes has made everyone’s life better. His economic models and suggestions have reduced the negative effects of the business cycle. Keynes has shown that there are real tools that the government can use to make life better, and to protect the economy from itself. If Smith saw the government as a bumbling ox, better suited for a cage, then Keynes fitted the ox with a plow and put it to work.

### 10. Formal — what he finds persuasive

First of all, I find Smith’s invisible hand very persuasive. The invisible hand is a great analogy for how societies got started and how they can be maintained with almost zero government intervention. Smith’s analogy of the invisible hand explains how differing parties, who only act in their self interest, can actually improve society more than a centralized, command and control regime. History proved this to be true: communist economies have failed, with many of them, like Russia and China, turning to capitalism.

Second, I find Keynes’s focus on macroeconomic tools to improve the short run to be almost as compelling as Smith’s invisible hand. In fact, Keynes was able to create effective, new strategies to help dampen the impact of business cycles. His focus on the short run has certainly protected many innocent people who would have otherwise been crushed by economic downturns. Keynes gave us all a new looking glass to clearly see how the aggregate economic world functions. Keynes ended up stealing part of the show from the invisible hand and gave it back to the government.

- Fake humility: "As someone who is passionate about…"

- Replacing his hedges with confidence. "I think" is not "I've learned". "Seems like" is not "It's clear".
